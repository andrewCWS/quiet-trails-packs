# Story 29 — The Stone That Skipped Eight

**Matrix:** story 39 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Activities · **Arc:** Race
**Stretch words:** angle, spin, surface
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** Billy could not skip a stone.

He had thrown, by his own count, more than forty. Every single one had gone out in a fine arc, landed with a solid *plunk*, and gone straight to the bottom like a stone, which is what it was.

Rori could skip a stone. Rori had never been taught, had never thought about it, and got two or three skips most throws with a bent arm and no apparent effort whatsoever.

"You're doing it wrong," said Rori helpfully.

"I know I'm doing it wrong. That's the whole *problem*."

So Billy sat down on the bank and did the Billy thing, which is to stop throwing and start watching.

He watched Rori throw six.

And here's what he noticed, in order, and it took him about ten minutes.

**One: the stone.** Rori was picking flat ones. Not just flattish — properly flat, with two parallel faces, about as wide as her palm and not much thicker than a biscuit. Billy had been picking whatever was nearest and some of them were nearly round.

A flat stone works because when it hits, it doesn't go in. It planes — it rides up on the **surface** the way a hand goes up if you stick it out of a car window at an angle.

**Two: the angle.** Rori's stones were hitting the water almost flat, but not quite — tipped up very slightly at the front. That small tip is what makes the water push it up instead of swallowing it. Too steep and it dives. Completely flat and it slaps and stops.

The number people usually land on is about twenty degrees, and nobody measures it. You feel it.

**Three: low.** Rori was throwing from about the height of her knee, out and along, nearly parallel with the water. Billy had been throwing from his shoulder, which sends the stone down onto the water instead of across it.

**Four, and this is the one Billy hadn't seen at all: the spin.**

Rori was flicking her finger as she let go, and the stone was coming off her hand rotating fast, like a tiny record.

That **spin** is doing something quite clever. A spinning object resists being tipped over — it's the same reason a moving bike stays up and a stationary one doesn't. So the stone stays flat through every bounce instead of tumbling and catching an edge. Without spin, you get one skip. With spin, you get all of them.

Billy went and found a proper stone. He crouched right down. He tipped the front up a fraction. And he flicked.

Four.

He made a noise that Skye described later, at length, to several people.

Then he got two. Then five. Then one, then a *plunk*, then six.

And somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, on a stone he'd been saving in his pocket since before lunch, with the water flat and no wind, he got eight.

Everybody saw it. That's the important part — Rori saw it, and Skye saw it, and Adi was facing the right way and said "again," which counts.

Eight small rings going away across the green water, getting closer together, and then a little skitter of tiny ones at the end that Billy insisted were skips and Rori insisted were not, and which the family will now argue about for the rest of its life.

Rori got seven later that day and Billy has never conceded that it happened.

**ECHO:** Nine.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing on the rocks a bit further along. That's Echo. Nobody had seen her arrive, because nobody ever does.

**ECHO:** A man got nine here. Years ago. He shouted about it for some time.

**NARRATOR:** "Can you do it?" said Billy.

**ECHO:** No hands.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** I can do the *sound*, though. I've got the plunk and I've got the skipping one and I've got the man shouting.

**NARRATOR:** "Do the man shouting."

**ECHO:** No.

Here's a mission, and it's a beating-yourself one, not a beating-anybody-else one.

Find the flattest stone you can. Get right down low. Tip the front up a whisker, and flick your finger as it leaves your hand.

Count your skips out loud. Then throw again and beat it.

Not somebody else's number. Yours. Whatever you got last time, get one more.

And if you get eight, you have to shout, because that's the rule.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound at all, which under the circumstances was pointed.
