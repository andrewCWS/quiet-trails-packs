# Story 50 — What We Took Home

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Going home · **Arc:** Voyage & return
**Stretch words:** souvenir, ordinary, remember
**Feeling:** not named — the last story shouldn't diagnose anybody
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo's last appearance, and the last mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** About an hour into the drive home, Rori put her hand in her pocket and found it empty.

She'd brought three things to Ganguddy. A torch, a book about horses, and a rock — grey, about the size of a plum, with a white line running all the way round the middle. Her rock. The one she'd said could come and meet the other rocks.

The torch was in the door pocket. The book was under Billy.

The rock was at the bottom of the river.

Adi had taken it out of Rori's bag on the second day, carried it down to the water in her red bag, held it up, said "bye," and dropped it in. Everybody had watched. It had seemed, at the time, extremely funny.

Rori sat with her hand in her empty pocket and did not find it funny at all for about six kilometres.

"You can get another rock," said Billy.

"It's not the same rock."

"It's the same *sort* of rock."

"It is *not the same rock*."

And she was right, and Billy knew she was right, and stopped.

Here's what a **souvenir** is supposed to be. It's a thing you bring back so that later, when you're somewhere else and everything is normal again, you can hold it and get a bit of the place back. A shell. A stone. A ticket. Something small enough for a pocket that's stuffed full of somewhere else.

The trouble is that it hardly ever works. You get home and put it on a shelf, and after about two weeks it stops being from anywhere and turns into a rock on a shelf. Which is why every house has a drawer with four shells in it that nobody can remember collecting.

Rori was quiet for a long time. The paddocks went by. The blue came back into the far hills.

And then she said: "Can we go to the bit where the wall is? Next time."

"The weir?"

"Yeah. Because I only saw it from the top. And I want to see the water going over from underneath."

Somebody said yes, next time.

And then Skye started, and did not stop for forty minutes, and what she did was list things. The fog on the water that nobody believed in. The log with the skink under it. The number of layers in the rock, which was still in dispute. The night they all stood in the dark laughing like idiots at a tree. The scribble that went round the trunk one and a half times. The thing that made a noise on the second night that they never identified and never will.

Billy joined in with corrections. Rori joined in with better versions. Adi said "bird" at appropriate moments and once at an inappropriate one, which got the biggest laugh of the drive.

By the time they hit the bitumen, Rori had stopped thinking about the rock.

Here's the thing about that.

Everything they were saying — every single item on Skye's enormous list — had come home with them. Free. Without a pocket. Nobody had to remember to pack the fog.

That's the **ordinary** miracle of going somewhere: the stuff you actually keep is the stuff you can't carry. The rock was never going to work. Three days of arguing about how many layers were in a rock is going to work for the rest of their lives.

And somewhere back behind them, in the leaf litter under the trees, on the day they left, there had been a voice.

**ECHO:** You're going.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail, standing very still, watching the car get packed. That's Echo. She'd been around all morning.

"Will you be here next time?" Rori had said.

**ECHO:** I'll be here. You might not find me. That's not the same thing.

**NARRATOR:** "How do I find you?"

**ECHO:** Same as always. Stop moving. Stop talking. Wait longer than you think is sensible.

You did it four times this week. You'll do it more next time, because you're better at it now.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched twice at the leaves. Found something. Ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's the last mission, and it's not for here. It's for when you get home.

Find one person who wasn't with you. A grandparent. A friend. Somebody at school.

Tell them one thing. Not the whole trip — nobody wants the whole trip. One thing. The best one.

And here's the hard part: you have to pick. You can't tell them all of it, so you have to decide which one it was.

That's the mission. Choose the thing you'd tell somebody, and go and tell them.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound at all, which for a bird who can make every sound in the world is a very good joke, and always will be.

Rori told her grandmother about the fog she never actually saw.

She'd heard Skye describe it eleven times by then, and honestly, by the end, it was a much better fog.
