# Story 11 — Adi Names Everything

**Matrix:** story 11 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Refrain · **Key kid:** Adi · **Category:** Birds & animals
**Stretch words:** none — this one is a shouting story
**Feeling:** none named. It's a happy one.
**Word count:** ~700 (~4:00). Refrain stories are short on purpose.
**Refrain:** Adi says the animal. Everybody checks. Adi is right.

---

**NARRATOR:** Nobody believes Adi.

That's not unkind. It's just that Adi is two, and Adi says "bird" about clouds, and "dog" about a bag on a fence post, and once said "cow" about a rock, so a certain amount of not-believing has built up over time.

It is also, as this trip proved, a mistake.

Somewhere past the last town, with the paddocks going by and everybody quiet, Adi said:

"Horse."

Nobody looked.

"*Horse.*"

"Adi, there's no —" said Rori, and then there was a horse. Brown. Standing under a tree with one back leg resting the way horses do, exactly where Adi's finger was pointing.

"Horse," Adi agreed.

Right. One each.

A bit further on, past a dam with a dead tree in it:

"Duck."

Everybody looked. There were eleven of them, black, sitting on the water like they'd been put there.

"They're not ducks, they're —" said Billy.

They were ducks. Pacific black ducks, which are ducks.

"Duck," said Adi.

Then a long stretch of nothing but fence and grass, and Rori started a game of I Spy that lasted four minutes and ended in an argument about whether a cloud counts as a thing.

Then:

"Bird!"

Which is Adi's most-used word, and the one nobody ever checks. But Skye happened to be looking the right way at the right moment, and there it was — enormous, sitting on a fence post with its shoulders hunched up, watching the grass. Brown, with a beak like something out of a museum.

"That's an *eagle*," said Skye. "That's an actual eagle, that's a wedge-tailed eagle, that's the biggest bird in the whole country, that's —"

"Bird," said Adi.

Which is, technically, all of that, and shorter.

Then the road went to gravel and everything got rattly, and for a while there was nothing but dust.

Then, at the edge of the trees, in the last of the light:

"Kangaroo."

Everybody looked. And this is the one Adi got wrong — except she didn't, quite.

It was smaller than a kangaroo and darker, almost black, with a shorter face, and it moved with its head low and its tail out straight behind it. It watched the car go past and did not run.

That's a swamp wallaby. Not a kangaroo. A cousin of one — same family, different animal — and honestly, at two years old, in a moving car, at dusk, from behind: close enough that nobody in that car was in a position to be smug about it.

"Wallaby," said Dad.

Adi considered this. "Wallaby," said Adi.

And then, about a minute later, quietly, to herself, as if filing it somewhere:

"Wallaby."

Here's the thing about Adi's naming. She isn't guessing. She's spotting. Two-year-olds are extremely good at noticing that something moved, because everything is new and nothing is background yet. The rest of us stopped seeing the paddocks about an hour ago. Adi is still looking at every single one.

So the score, by the time they got to camp, was: horse, duck, eagle, one wallaby with a correction, four cows, and a cat that nobody else ever saw and which we are choosing to believe in.

Everyone else got nothing, because everyone else was looking at the back of a seat.

So here's a mission, and it's a shouting one.

Watch out the window. Not at anything in particular — just out.

The first three animals you see, you have to name out loud. Any animal. A cow counts. A dog in a ute counts. A bird you can't identify absolutely counts, and you can just shout "bird" like Adi does, because Adi is right more often than anybody expected.

Three animals. Say them loud enough that the driver hears.

And if somebody says you're wrong — make them look first.
