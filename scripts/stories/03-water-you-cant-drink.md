# Story 3 — Water You Can't Drink

**Matrix:** story 3 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Trip prep · **Arc:** How-it-works
**Stretch words:** supply, invisible, boil
**Feeling:** relieved, named once
**Word count:** ~1030
**Note:** story 1 says *bring water*. This one is the why — the river is not
the problem people assume it is, and boiling is the answer.

---

**NARRATOR:** The river at Ganguddy is green.

Not murky green. Not swampy green. It's the green of a bottle held up to the sun, and it goes on between two walls of orange rock for longer than you can see, and when the wind stops it turns into a mirror with trees in it.

It is, without question, the best-looking water any of them had ever stood next to.

"Can I drink it?" said Rori.

"No," said Billy.

"Can I drink it if I use a cup?"

"No."

"Can I drink it if I —"

"*No,*" said Billy, who had thought about this a great deal, on the way, for four hours.

Because Billy had counted the bottles.

That was his job, and he had given it to himself. Before they left he had stood in the driveway and counted every container of water going into the car — the big square one with the tap, two smaller ones, and a stack of bottles — and worked out that this was the whole **supply**. Not most of it. All of it. There was no tap at Ganguddy. There was no shop. What was in the car was what there was.

He'd written the number on the back of his hand. It had mostly worn off, but he still knew it.

So when he walked past the big square container that afternoon and heard it make the wrong noise — a light, hollow, echoey noise instead of a heavy sloshing one — he stopped walking.

He picked it up. It came up far too easily.

The tap on the front was turned, very slightly, to the open side. Somebody had filled a cup that morning and not quite closed it, and it had been quietly emptying itself into the dirt ever since, and the dirt had drunk the lot.

Billy stood there holding an enormous empty box and did some very fast arithmetic.

"Okay," he said, out loud, to nobody. "Okay. Okay."

Now here's what he did next, and it's the bit worth remembering. He didn't shout, and he didn't hide it. He went and found a grown-up and said the true thing straight away: *I think we've lost most of the water.*

Which is how they all ended up down at the green river ten minutes later, with a saucepan.

"So we CAN drink it," said Rori.

"We can drink it *after,*" said Billy.

The river water went into the saucepan and the saucepan went onto the fire, and while they waited, this got explained, and it is worth knowing.

Water that looks perfectly clean can carry things far too small to see — tiny living things that get in from animals, from birds, from soil washed in by rain. They're **invisible**. That's the whole trouble with them: your eyes tell you the water is fine, and your eyes are not equipped for the question.

But those tiny things can't survive being hot. So you **boil** the water — properly boil it, big rolling bubbles, not just steam — and after a minute of that, the invisible things are gone and the water is safe. It's one of the oldest tricks people have, and it still works, and it needs nothing but fire and a pot.

"So we boil it," said Rori.

"We boil it."

"And *then* I can drink the river."

"Then you can drink the river."

Rori considered this. "That's better than a tap," she decided.

The saucepan took a long time. Water always takes longer to boil when somebody is watching it, which is a real thing and not a joke. Skye filled the time by explaining to everyone how boiling worked, including the parts she had just been told, and including one part she invented, which was that the bubbles were the invisible things escaping. That isn't right. It was a good guess, though, and nobody said it wasn't.

Adi sat on a rock with her cup and waited with enormous patience for a two-year-old, which is about four minutes.

And when it had boiled, and been left to go cool, and finally got poured — Billy drank first, because he'd found the problem, and that seemed fair to everyone.

There's a feeling you get when a thing you were quietly frightened about turns out to be fixable. Everything in your shoulders comes down about an inch. That feeling is called being **relieved**, and Billy sat down on the rock next to Adi and felt it all the way to his feet.

Then he went and turned the tap on the empty container to closed, and then checked it again, and then, a bit later, checked it one more time.

The **supply** lasted the rest of the trip. They boiled river water twice more, for the billy and for the washing up, and each time Rori announced that she was drinking a river, which she was.

So here's a mission for whoever's got water in the car with them.

Count it. Every bottle, every container, all of it, and say the number out loud.

Then work out the sneaky part: how many of those are for *drinking*, and how many are really for cooking, and washing hands, and the dog?

Because the number you counted first is never quite the number you've got.
