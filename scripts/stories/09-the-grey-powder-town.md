# Story 9 — The Grey Powder Town

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Water & the weir · **Arc:** Time-slip
**Stretch words:** concrete, factory, century
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1080

---

**NARRATOR:** You see the chimney before you see anything else.

It comes up out of the paddocks on the left, thin and grey and far too tall, and it doesn't look like it belongs to the hills or the sheep or the road. It looks like somebody put it down in the wrong story.

"What's *that*," said Rori.

Nobody answered straight away, because nobody in the car was entirely sure, and there is a rule in families that the person who answers first has to be right afterwards.

Around it were buildings, and the buildings were the same colour as the chimney, and the paddocks near the buildings were the same colour too — a pale, dusty grey that had settled on the grass and stayed there.

"It's a **factory**," said Billy.

"For what?"

"For grey."

That's not far off, as it turns out.

The chimney belongs to a cement works. Cement is the grey powder that goes into **concrete**, and concrete is what almost everything hard and flat in your life is made of — footpaths, bridges, the floor of the garage, the pipe the water comes through, the wall you lean on outside the shops. It doesn't look like much on its own. It's the most boring-looking substance in the world. And then you add water and it turns into stone.

To make cement, you need limestone, and you need to cook it. Really cook it. Hotter than a fire, hotter than an oven, in a spinning drum the length of a street. Then you grind what comes out into powder.

"And that's what the town's *for*?" said Rori.

Yes. That's exactly what the town is for. Kandos wasn't a place that grew slowly with people arriving one at a time. It was built, more or less all at once, about a **century** ago, for the people who worked at the cement works. The wide main street, the big buildings, the houses in rows — all of it exists because somebody found the right rock and decided to cook it.

Rori pressed her face to the window and watched the chimney go past, and get smaller, and slide behind a hill.

And then the road went on, and the paddocks turned to bush, and the bush got closer to the car, and about half an hour later they were standing at the edge of the greenest water any of them had ever seen, with orange rock going up out of it on both sides.

Which is where the time-slip comes in, and it's the good bit.

That water is there because of the chimney.

The cement works needed water. An enormous amount of it — a factory that cooks rock all day is thirsty in a way that's hard to imagine. So the people who built the town went out into the bush, found a place where the Cudgegong River ran through a narrow gap in the rock, and built a wall across it.

The wall is called a weir. It was finished around ninety-odd years ago. And once it was there, the river couldn't get past, so it did the only thing water can do — it backed up, and spread out, and filled all the low ground behind the wall, and turned a river into the long still green place that people now drive four hours to swim in.

So when Rori jumped in — and Rori was first in, because Rori is always first in — she was swimming in a water supply. A hundred-year-old **factory** decision, gone quietly beautiful.

"So it's not real?" said Rori, treading water.

Which is a fair question, and the answer is: it's completely real. The rock is real and two hundred million years old and was here long before anybody. The turtles are real. The platypus is real and does not care why the water is deep. The lyrebird in the leaves has no idea there is such a thing as cement.

The only thing that's a hundred years old is the *shape* of the water. Everything living in it just moved into a good spot.

Billy, who had been thinking, said: "So there's a wall. There's a wall *somewhere*."

There is. It's down the far end, and you can walk to it, and it is exactly as unromantic as a wall — grey, blunt, with water sliding over it in a flat sheet and falling into the ordinary river on the other side, where the river goes back to being a river again and carries on to wherever it was always going.

Standing on top of it, you can look one way at a hundred years of held-back water full of turtles, and the other way at the river doing what it did before anybody arrived. Same water. One wall.

Rori went and looked at the wall twice.

Then she found something better, which was a lump of old **concrete** at the edge of the bush with a bit of rusted wire coming out of it, going soft at the corners, with moss on the top and a plant growing out of a crack. Something built, being slowly taken back.

"That's from the chimney people," she said, which was probably not true, and is a very good guess.

So here's a mission for later, wherever you end up.

Find something made of **concrete**. It won't be hard — it's everywhere, that's the whole point of it.

Now find the oldest bit of concrete you can. Look for the cracks, the moss, the rounded-off corners, the plant that's decided to live in it.

And then work out what it was *for*, before it was just a lump. Because somebody put it there on purpose, and they had a reason, and quite often the reason is long gone and the concrete isn't.
