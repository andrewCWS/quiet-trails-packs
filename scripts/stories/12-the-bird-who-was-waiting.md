# Story 12 — The Bird Who Was Waiting

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Birds
**Stretch words:** mimic (x3), patient (x1 — it's the story's point, not vocabulary)
**Feeling:** frustrated, named once
**Word count:** ~1010 (~5:00 at 200 wpm)
**Voices:** NARRATOR carries all four kids in reported speech. ECHO is the only
separate voice — being a different voice is how you know she isn't a child.

---

**NARRATOR:** The road had been bumping for a long time. Not the smooth kind of bumping. The kind where the tyres go *crunch crunch crunch* on little stones, and the dust comes up behind the car like a brown ghost.

Then the crunching stopped.

"Are we here?" said Rori. "Are we *here?*"

They were here.

The doors opened and four kids came out of two cars like water out of a bottle. Rori first, because Rori was always first. Then Billy, half a second behind, because Billy did not enjoy being second. Then Skye, who took her time. Then Adi, who had to be lifted, and who was already pointing at something.

"Bird," said Adi.

Nobody looked. Adi pointed at a lot of things, and said "bird" about most of them.

Rori spun in a circle to see everything at once. There was water, long and still and dark green, with orange rock standing up out of it like a wall somebody had built and then forgotten. There were trees with smooth pale bark. There was a smell she didn't have a word for yet, dry and sweet and a bit like pencils.

And down on the ground, in the crackly brown leaves under the trees, something was scratching.

"There's something there," whispered Rori.

It was a bird. A big one, bigger than a chicken, brown all over, with a tail like a long untidy feather duster. It was kicking backwards with its feet, throwing leaves up in the air, digging for something.

Rori's chest went fast. Her legs already knew what they wanted to do.

She ran.

The bird did not fly away. That was the strange part. It *ran*. It put its head down and went off through the leaf litter, quick as a cat, and then there was a bush, and then there was nothing at all.

Rori stopped where it had been. There was a little dug-up patch, and a scatter of leaves, and one grey feather, and that was it.

"I saw it," said Rori. "I *saw* it."

"Well, you scared it," said Billy.

"I didn't *mean* to," said Rori.

Her face went hot. There's a feeling you get when you wanted something very much and you were the reason it went away. It sits in your throat and makes you want to kick a rock. That feeling is called being **frustrated**.

She kicked a rock. It was a good rock. It didn't help much.

"It didn't fly," said Skye, quietly.

"What?" said Billy.

"It ran," said Skye. "Birds fly. That one ran."

Skye had been standing still the whole time, which is why she'd seen it properly. She usually did.

"Okay," said Billy. "Okay. So if it runs instead of flying, it can't get away fast. So it must hide instead. So if we don't move, it doesn't know where we are."

Billy said all of this out loud, which was how Billy thought. Then he sat down in the dirt without checking whether the dirt was clean, which for Billy was a very big deal.

"Everyone sit down," said Billy. "Nobody talk."

"How long?" said Rori.

"Until it comes back," said Billy.

"*That's not a number,*" said Rori.

But she sat down.

And here is the thing about sitting down in the bush and not talking. At first it is boring. Then it is *very* boring. Rori counted to sixty in her head twice and thought about giving up.

And then the bush started to fill in around her.

A tick-tick-tick from somewhere in a branch. Water slapping softly on rock. Wind moving the top of one tree and not the others. A tiny scuffle that could have been a lizard. All of it had been there the whole time. She just hadn't been still enough to let it in.

That's what being **patient** actually is. Not waiting for nothing. Waiting until you notice there was never nothing.

Then a kookaburra laughed. Right there, close.

Adi's head came up. "Bird," said Adi.

Except there was no kookaburra. Skye was looking in exactly the right place, and there was no kookaburra there at all.

There was a brown bird with an untidy tail, standing very still in the leaves, looking straight back at her.

And then it made the sound of a whipbird. And then a magpie. And then, unmistakably, the sound of a car door slamming.

"That's, that's not *fair,*" said Billy.

It is completely fair, and it is completely real. That bird is a lyrebird, and a lyrebird is the best **mimic** in the world. To **mimic** something means to copy it exactly. Most of what a lyrebird sings isn't its own song at all. It's other birds. Dozens of them. And here's the strangest part: lyrebirds don't learn those sounds from the birds. They learn them from *other lyrebirds*. A sound can get passed down from bird to bird to bird for years and years, long after the thing that made it has gone.

Which means a lyrebird is a bit like a very old song that walks around on legs. A **mimic** of a mimic of a mimic.

Then the bird spoke, and it did not sound like a bird at all.

**ECHO:** You ran at me.

**NARRATOR:** Four children fell over.

**ECHO:** I'm Echo. You ran at me, and I left. That's how it works. I don't come for loud. I come for still.

**NARRATOR:** "I'm sorry," said Rori.

**ECHO:** I know. You'll do it again. But you'll do it less.

Here's a mission for tonight. Before dinner, sit down somewhere and be still for one whole minute. Don't look for anything. Just let it come in.

I'll know if you cheat.

**NARRATOR:** And then Echo was gone, without a sound, which for a bird who can make every sound in the world is a very good joke.
