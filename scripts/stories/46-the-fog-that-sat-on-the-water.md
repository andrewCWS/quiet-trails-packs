# Story 46 — The Fog That Sat on the Water

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** Weather & sky · **Arc:** Patience
**Stretch words:** valley, condense, dawn
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1050
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** Skye woke up early because she was cold, which is the only reason anybody ever wakes up early on purpose by accident.

It was the last morning. The tent was cold. Her nose was cold. Outside it was that pale grey that isn't night any more but hasn't committed to being day.

She got up, because there was nothing else to do about being cold, and she put on somebody else's jumper, and she went down to the water.

And the water was gone.

Not gone gone. There was still a lake-shaped hole between the rocks where a lake ought to be. But sitting on top of it, about as thick as a person is tall, was a flat white layer of cloud. It wasn't up in the sky. It was *on the water*, lying there like something asleep, moving very slowly, with the orange rock walls sticking up out of it on both sides and the tops of some drowned trees poking through.

You couldn't see the far end. You couldn't see across. You could see about six metres and then white.

Skye did not say anything, which is worth noting, because Skye says something about everything. She sat down on a cold rock and watched it and said nothing for a very long time.

Here's why it happens, and it happens most in a place shaped like this one.

Cold air is heavier than warm air. So at night, when the ground and the rocks lose their heat, the cold air slides downhill — off the ridges, down the slopes, and pools in the bottom of the **valley** like water filling a bath. That's why the campsite is colder than the hill behind it, and it's why the frost gets there first.

Meanwhile the lake has been holding onto its heat all night, because water is slow about everything. So by morning you have cold air lying directly on top of warmish water.

And when warm damp air touches something cold, the water in it stops being invisible and turns into droplets. It **condenses**. Millions of them, hanging in the air, too light to fall.

That's fog. It's not smoke and it's not steam. It's ordinary air that got cold enough to stop hiding what it was carrying.

Then the sun comes up over the ridge, and warms the air a degree or two, and every one of those droplets goes back to being invisible, and the whole thing vanishes in about twenty minutes and takes the lake with it — which is to say it gives the lake back.

Skye watched the entire thing.

She watched a bird fly into the white and disappear completely. She watched a fish, or something, make a ring on the surface that she could hear but not see. She watched the light change from grey to gold along the top of the rock wall, coming down it like something being poured, until it reached the fog and the fog started to come apart in strands.

And she watched the far end of the lake arrive back — trees first, then rocks, then water — as if somebody were rubbing out the white with a finger.

By the time everybody else got up, there was no fog at all and no evidence of any and nothing to show anyone.

"There was a *cloud on the lake*," said Skye.

"Sure," said Rori.

"There *was.*"

"Sure."

This is the fundamental problem with getting up early, and nobody has ever solved it.

But somebody had seen her. From the leaf litter behind the rock, a voice she recognised.

**ECHO:** I saw it too.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing very still under the trees. That's Echo. She turns up for people who've gone quiet, and Skye had been quiet for forty minutes, which for Skye is close to a world record.

**ECHO:** They think you made it up.

**NARRATOR:** "They do."

**ECHO:** They'll believe you next time. Not this time.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched twice at the leaves, found something, ate it, and looked up at the perfectly clear lake.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it costs you something, which most of them don't.

Get up once — just once — before the sun does. Set an alarm, or ask somebody big to wake you, or be cold like she was.

Go outside and don't do anything. Don't look for the fog. It might not be there. Frost might be there instead, or nothing at all except birds starting up one at a time.

Stay until the light hits the top of something. Then you can go back to bed.

**NARRATOR:** And then Echo was gone, without a sound, into a morning that had nothing left in it to show anybody.

Skye told the story in the car, four hours, twice.

By the second telling the fog was taller.
