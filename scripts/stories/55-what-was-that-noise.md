**NARRATOR:** It happened at about eleven o'clock, and it woke everybody except Adi.

(sfx: boobook)

Two notes. Low, soft, close together, coming out of the dark somewhere past the trees. Then a pause long enough that you started to think you'd imagined it. Then the same two notes again, from a slightly different place.

Skye was awake instantly, in the way you are when a sound arrives inside a dream and turns out to be real.

There's a tight, listening, completely still feeling that arrives when you don't know what something is and it's dark. It stops you breathing properly. That feeling is called being **nervous**, and Skye had it in both hands, which were holding the top of her sleeping bag.

"Did you hear it," she whispered.

"Mm," said Rori, asleep.

"Rori. *Did you hear it.*"

(sfx: boobook)

Again. Closer, or maybe not closer — sound does strange things at night and it's very hard to tell.

Now. Here's the useful thing to know about noises at night, and it isn't "don't worry about it."

Almost everything that makes a noise in the Australian bush at night is small, busy, and completely uninterested in you. The bush after dark isn't empty and it isn't dangerous — it's *crowded*, with animals that are **nocturnal**, which means they sleep in the day and do all their living at night. They're not hiding from you. They just work different hours.

The difficulty is that you can't see any of them, so your brain — which is very good at its job and slightly over-enthusiastic — has to guess what made the sound. And in the dark, with nothing to check against, it always guesses big.

So you do the thing that fixes it, which is that you **identify** the noise.

Not "ignore the noise". Identify it. Because a sound with a name attached is a completely different sound.

Skye got out of the tent, which took nerve, and found that one of the grown-ups was already sitting by the last of the fire with a cup of tea, because grown-ups are often awake at eleven o'clock and this is one of the great unfair secrets of childhood.

They sat and listened.

(sfx: boobook)

"That," said the grown-up, "is a boobook."

A boobook is an owl. A small one — smaller than you'd think, about the size of a magpie — and it is the most common owl in the country and it says its own name. Boo-book. Two notes, over and over, sometimes for hours. It's hunting: sitting still on a branch, watching the ground for mice and beetles and moths, calling to say where it is.

"It's an *owl*," said Skye.

It's an owl.

And that was that. The exact same sound, arriving in the exact same dark, was now completely fine — because it had a name and a size and a job.

They stayed up and did the rest of them.

There was a rustle in the leaves near the tent that turned out to be, almost certainly, a bandicoot, because bandicoots dig little cone-shaped holes and there were fresh ones in the morning.

There was a thump, then another thump, then nothing — a swamp wallaby moving off, because a wallaby's warning is to bang its back feet on the ground.

There was a sound like somebody dragging a chair across a roof, which is a possum, and which is a noise so ridiculous for the size of the animal making it that once you know, you laugh instead of freezing.

There was a long series of hollow knocks from down near the water, which they never worked out.

And there was, once, a very close and unmistakable sound of a car door slamming, which made both of them sit bolt upright, and which was not a car door.

Skye started grinning before she'd even turned round.

**ECHO:** I do that one when I'm bored.

**NARRATOR:** "That's not fair," said Skye, delighted.

**ECHO:** No.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it's for the middle of the night, which is the best time for a mission.

When something wakes you up — and something will — don't put a torch on. Lie there and listen to it.

Then name three night sounds. Any three. You don't have to be right. Say what you think each one is, out loud, in the morning, and see who agrees.

And keep one that nobody can name. Every camp should have one of those.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound, which was showing off.

Their unnamed one was the hollow knocking down near the water.

They still don't know.
