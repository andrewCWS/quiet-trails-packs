# Story 10 — The Road That Isn't Sealed

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** The drive · **Arc:** Overcoming fear
**Stretch words:** sealed, corrugated, surface
**Feeling:** brave, named once
**Word count:** ~1070

---

**NARRATOR:** The road changed, and Skye stopped talking.

That's the part everybody noticed, afterwards. Not the road — the quiet. Skye had been talking since the last town. She had done the horses in the paddock, and a gate that was open when it should have been shut, and a long section about whether a wombat could beat a dog in a race, with reasons.

And then the sound under the car went from a hum to a rattle, and the talking stopped as if somebody had turned a tap.

Here's what happened to the road.

Up until then it had been **sealed**. That's the black stuff — bitumen, tar, whatever your family calls it — poured on and rolled flat, so the tyres have nothing to argue with. A sealed road makes almost no noise at all. You stop hearing it about four minutes into a trip and never hear it again.

Then the black stopped, and the road became what roads are underneath: dirt and small stones, graded flat by a machine every so often, and then not flat again shortly afterwards.

It sounds completely different. There's a hiss of stones spraying up under the car. There's a low drumming as the tyres cross **corrugations**, which are the ripples that form across a gravel road all by themselves, like sand at the beach. And there's the occasional bang of one stone getting flicked up and hitting something underneath, which sounds much worse than it is and makes everybody jump.

The car also moves differently. It shifts a bit, side to side. It takes the bends more slowly. The dust comes up behind in a long brown flag and hangs there.

"Is it broken," said Skye. Very fast, all one word, not really a question.

"The car?"

"The *road.*"

"Nope," said Dad. "This is just what it looks like when nobody's put the top on it."

Skye did not find this as reassuring as it was meant to be.

There's a feeling that arrives when something familiar starts behaving in a way you weren't expecting. It's not exactly fear. It's more like the floor of you going slightly loose. Skye had both hands on the edge of her seat and was watching the back of the driver's headrest instead of the window, which is what she does when she's decided not to look at something.

And this is where Billy did a Billy thing. He didn't tell her it was fine. He gave her a job.

"Skye. What's it doing now?"

"What?"

"The sound. What's it doing."

Skye listened, because being asked a question about the world is nearly impossible to ignore.

"...It's the drumming one."

"Okay. Tell me when it changes."

So she did. And it turned out the road had a whole language, and Skye — who notices everything and says all of it — was extremely good at reading it.

The drumming meant corrugations, and that meant Dad slowed down a bit, because the trick with corrugations is that they get worse and then better again depending on how fast you go.

The hiss meant loose gravel on top, and that meant the **surface** was newly graded.

A sudden smooth stretch meant clay instead of stone, and clay means something completely different when it's wet.

A rattle of bigger rocks under the wheel arches meant they were near a floodway, where water crosses the road when it rains and takes the small stones away with it, leaving the big ones behind.

And the best one: a long, low, almost musical rumble, which meant a cattle grid — a set of steel bars across the road that a car can drive over and a cow can't walk on, so the paddock stays shut without anybody getting out to open a gate.

"THAT ONE," said Skye, and then made everybody wait for the next one, and there were four.

By the time the road turned in under the trees, Skye had a running commentary going that nobody could have stopped with a stick, and she had entirely forgotten about being frightened, which is generally how it goes.

There's a straight-backed, slightly surprised feeling that comes at the end of doing a thing you thought you couldn't. That feeling is called being **brave**, and the odd part about it is that you almost never notice it at the time. You notice it about ten minutes later, when you realise you're not holding onto the seat any more.

Skye noticed at the cattle grid.

"I wasn't scared of that," she announced, to the car in general.

"No," said Billy.

"I *wasn't.*"

"I know."

And that night, when somebody asked what the drive was like, Skye explained the difference between corrugations and loose gravel to two adults who had not asked, in considerable detail, twice.

So here's a mission, and this one is for the road, so get ready.

Close your eyes. Actually close them — this only works with them shut.

Now listen to the road. Just the road, under everything else.

Whoever's driving: say nothing. Wait.

And the moment the sound changes — the second the hum goes to a rattle, or the rattle goes to a drum, or the drum goes to that low musical rumble of a cattle grid — everybody shouts.

Then open your eyes and find out what you were driving over.
