# Story 19 — The Handwriting on the Tree

**Matrix:** story 15 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** Insects & small things · **Arc:** Mystery
**Stretch words:** tunnel, hatch, retrace
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** Skye found the writing on the second morning.

The tree was smooth and pale, the way gums go when they've dropped their old bark — almost white, almost soft-looking, the sort of trunk you want to put your cheek against because it looks cool.

And all over it, from about knee height up to higher than anyone could reach, somebody had written.

Not words. Lines. Thin dark squiggles that wandered up and across and doubled back on themselves, went round in a loop, changed their minds, and then ran alongside their own earlier line for a while like two people walking together. Some were short. One went all the way round the trunk.

"Somebody's *drawn* on it," said Skye. And then, because she is Skye: "Somebody's drawn on it and they've done a really long one and they've gone round the back, has anyone been round the back, there's more round the back —"

There was more round the back.

Rori tried to rub one off. It didn't rub off. It wasn't on the bark. It was *in* it.

"It's like handwriting," said Billy.

It is like handwriting. The tree is called a scribbly gum, and it is called that for exactly this reason, and the story of who does the writing is better than anything anyone guessed.

It's a caterpillar. Very small, and the grub of a little moth, and it lives its whole young life in the thin gap between the tree's old bark and its new bark.

Here's how the scribble happens.

The moth lays an egg in a crack in the bark. The egg **hatches**, and the tiny caterpillar starts eating its way along, in that narrow gap, making a **tunnel** as it goes. As it eats, it grows. So the tunnel starts out hair-thin and gets steadily wider, which is why the lines are thin at one end and fat at the other — you're looking at an animal getting bigger, drawn as a line.

And the doubling back? That's the strangest part. At some point the caterpillar turns around and **retraces** its own tunnel, eating along the same path it already made, because new food has grown there in the meantime. That's why the lines run beside themselves. That's why they loop.

Then the old bark falls off, the way gum bark does, and the tunnel that was hidden underneath is suddenly on the outside, dark against the pale new wood, where everybody can see it.

So the writing isn't writing. It's a record. Every scribble on that tree is the life story of one caterpillar, made public by accident, after it left.

Skye sat down at the bottom of the tree and did not talk for nearly a minute, which for Skye is a considerable event.

"So the tree's got everybody's *history* on it," she said.

Yes.

"So the long ones are the ones who lived the longest."

Roughly, yes.

"So this one — " she put her finger at the start of a thin line and followed it, up, round, back down, off to the left, and up again for a very long way, "— this one had a *big* time."

That one had a big time.

They spent the rest of the morning on it. Billy measured with a piece of string and then lost the piece of string. Rori found one that went round the trunk one and a half times and declared it the winner in a competition nobody had announced. Adi patted the tree and said "book," which was the single best thing anybody said all day.

And Skye found the one that mattered, which was a line that started, went about the length of a finger, and simply stopped. No fat end. No loop.

"That one didn't finish," she said.

"No."

"Something got it."

Probably. Or the bark got too dry, or the tunnel ran out of somewhere to go. The tree doesn't say. That's the thing about reading a wall of other people's stories — most of them don't have endings, and the ones that do, you can't hear.

Skye looked at that short line for a while longer than the others.

Then a voice came out of the leaf litter behind them.

**ECHO:** You've found the writing.

**NARRATOR:** There was a brown bird standing very still, with a tail like an untidy feather duster, watching them. That's Echo. She comes when people have stopped rushing about, and four children sitting under a tree reading it counts.

**ECHO:** I like this tree. I've heard a lot of things standing next to it.

**NARRATOR:** "Can you read them?" said Skye.

**ECHO:** No. I only do sounds. Nobody wrote these out loud.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched twice at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it needs good eyes.

Find a scribbly tree. Then find the longest scribble on it — the whole way, every loop, round the back if it goes round the back. Use a finger. Don't cheat by picking a fat one; fat isn't long.

Say how long you think it is. Then find somebody else's longest, and put them next to each other.

And when you've found the longest, go and find the shortest one too. Somebody should look at that one.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound at all.
