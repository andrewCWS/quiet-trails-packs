# Story 6 — Four Hours

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** The drive · **Arc:** Voyage & return
**Stretch words:** distance, halfway, journey
**Feeling:** impatient, named once
**Word count:** ~1010

---

**NARRATOR:** Eleven minutes into a four-hour drive, Rori asked how much longer.

"Four hours," said Dad.

"That's what you said *before.*"

"That's because it was four hours before, and now it's four hours minus eleven minutes, which is still, essentially, four hours."

Rori put her head back against the seat and made a noise like a tyre going down.

Here's the trouble with four hours. If you're five, four hours is not an amount of time. It's a word. It means *later*, and it means *not now*, and it means the same whether it's four hours or fourteen. You can't hold it. There's nothing to hold.

There's a squirming, kicking, can't-get-comfortable feeling that comes with waiting for something you badly want, and it lives in your legs. That feeling is called being **impatient**, and Rori had it in both legs and was working on getting it into her arms.

Billy, who was in the middle, had been quietly reading the dashboard for the last ten minutes.

"There's a number," he said.

"There's lots of numbers."

"No, there's a — the little one. It goes up." He leaned forward as far as the seatbelt would let him. "It went up while I was looking at it."

That's the odometer. Every car has one, and it has been counting since the day the car was new. Not hours. Not how far you've got left. Just kilometres, one at a time, adding up forever, and it never goes backwards and it never resets.

Which means the number on that dial is the whole **distance** the car has ever travelled. Every trip to the shops. Every time somebody drove somewhere and turned around and came home. All of it, added together, in one long number nobody ever reads.

"So how far is *ours*?" said Rori.

Billy did the maths on his fingers, got lost, and started again. "About two hundred and fifty."

"Two hundred and fifty *what*."

"Kilometres."

"Is that a lot?"

And this is where Billy did the useful thing, which he did not know was going to be the useful thing.

"It's about — " he said, "it's about a hundred and twenty-five there, and then a hundred and twenty-five more."

Rori sat up. "So there's a middle."

"There's a middle."

"There's a middle and we could *find* it."

There was, and they could. It's called being **halfway**, and it is the single most important place on any long drive, because it's the moment the drive stops getting longer and starts getting shorter. Before halfway, you're going away. After halfway, you're arriving. Nothing changes outside the window. Everything changes inside the car.

So they made it a job.

Billy wrote down the number on the odometer when they left, on the back of an envelope, in pencil. Then he added a hundred and twenty-five to it, which took a while and two goes, and that was the halfway number. Then everybody watched for it.

And while they watched, they counted towns.

This turned out to be harder than it sounds, because a town is not always obvious. A town has a sign, usually. But some places have a sign and four houses and a shed, and some have a sign and nothing at all except a name, and once there was a sign for a place and then, immediately, another sign saying you were leaving it.

"Does that count?"

"It had a *name*," said Skye. "If it's got a name it counts. Everything with a name counts. That's how names *work* — "

They counted nine. Then eleven. Then they lost the number and started again at the next one, and got to six.

Adi counted differently. Adi counted cows. Adi's count was better, because Adi's count was just "cow," said out loud, every single time, and it was always correct.

And somewhere out past a place with a name and a silo and a dog asleep in the shade, Billy shouted, because the number on the odometer matched the number on the envelope.

Halfway.

Everybody cheered, including Dad, who had been driving for two hours and needed it.

And the strange thing — the thing that's worth knowing about long **journeys** — is that the second half went quicker. It always does. Not really. The clock doesn't care. But once you know you're on the coming-back-down side of a thing, your legs stop kicking, and you start looking out the window instead of at the seat in front of you.

The road went from smooth to bumpy. The bumps turned to gravel. Somewhere in the last half-hour, Rori stopped asking how long, and started asking what the next town would be called.

So here's a mission for the car, and you can start it right now.

Count the towns. Every single one with a name on a sign, however small, however silly, however much it's only a shed.

Say the number out loud when you get there. And whoever's driving — tell them the number too. They've been counting something else entirely.
