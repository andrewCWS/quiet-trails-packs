# Story 20 — The Laughing Family

**Matrix:** story 22 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Birds · **Arc:** Trickster
**Stretch words:** territory, chorus, kingfisher
**Feeling:** not named in this one. It's a laughing story.
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.
**Sound:** the kookaburra plays three times — this is the one story where the
effect is the plot.

---

**NARRATOR:** They started before the sun did.

One bird, somewhere out past the tents, in the grey part of the morning where everything is the same colour. It began with a sort of low chuckle, like somebody remembering something funny, and then it went up, and up, and turned into a full laugh — enormous, ridiculous, echoing off the rocks.

(sfx: kookaburra)

And then a second bird answered from the other direction. And a third joined underneath. And for about twenty seconds the whole campsite was inside a noise that sounded exactly like a family of people falling about at a joke you weren't told.

Billy was awake instantly, in the way you are when a sound is genuinely enormous.

Rori sat up and shouted "WHAT," which added to it.

Adi, in the next bed, said "bird," and went back to sleep, because Adi is unbothered.

And then it stopped, all at once, the way it started, and there was nothing at all except one duck somewhere on the water.

"Was that a *monkey*?" said Rori.

It wasn't a monkey, although people have thought so for a very long time, and honestly, fair enough.

That's a kookaburra. Several of them. And nearly everything a person assumes about a kookaburra is slightly wrong, which is what made Billy's morning.

Start with the name. A kookaburra is a **kingfisher** — the biggest one in the world. Kingfishers are the little jewel-coloured birds that sit over water and dive in after fish. And a kookaburra is one of those, scaled up to the size of a football, that has given up on fish almost entirely.

It hunts on land. It sits still on a branch, dead still, looking down, and then drops on a lizard, or a beetle, or a mouse, or — and this is the one that impresses five-year-olds — a snake.

"A *snake,*" said Billy.

A snake.

Then there's the laugh, and the laugh isn't a laugh.

It's a **chorus**, and it's a family doing it together. Kookaburras live in groups, usually parents plus their grown-up young, and the young ones don't leave. They stay for years and help raise the next lot of chicks. The whole group holds one patch of bush between them, and the laugh is how they say so — this is our **territory**, we are all here, and there are more of us than you.

They do it at first light and again at dusk. If you hear one group start, listen for the answer: another family, further off, saying the same thing back.

Which meant that what woke Billy up wasn't a joke. It was a boundary being drawn, in sound, across the whole valley, by several households at once.

He thought that was the best thing he'd ever heard.

They found them after breakfast — two, sitting on a dead branch, doing nothing whatsoever with tremendous commitment. Up close a kookaburra is much bigger than you expect and much stiller. It doesn't fidget. It just watches the ground, with a beak on it like a pair of shears.

And then Rori laughed at them. On purpose. A big, fake, silly, kookaburra laugh.

And one of them laughed back.

(sfx: kookaburra)

Every single person there lost their minds.

They tried again. Billy tried, and got nothing. Skye tried, and got nothing, and explained at length why she'd got nothing, and got nothing again. Rori tried a second time and got an answer, and then a third time and got two answers.

Nobody knows what Rori was doing right. That's the honest end of that.

It might be that she started low and went up. It might be that she happened to leave the right size of gap. Or it might be that the birds had simply decided to answer that particular time and would have answered anybody, which is the most likely explanation and the least satisfying one.

(sfx: kookaburra)

Then, from down in the leaf litter, another voice, and this one was doing the kookaburra perfectly and wasn't a kookaburra at all.

**ECHO:** That's not how you do it.

**NARRATOR:** There was a brown bird in the leaves with a long untidy tail, standing very still. That's Echo. She copies things. It's what she's for.

**ECHO:** You went too high, too fast. They start slow. Like they're thinking about it.

**NARRATOR:** "Do it again," said Rori.

**ECHO:** No.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves and found something and ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission. It's an embarrassing one, so everybody has to do it, including the adults. Especially the adults.

At dawn or just on dark, when you hear them start — laugh back. Properly. As loud as they are. Start low, go up, and don't stop early because you feel silly.

You probably won't get an answer. That's fine. The bird is not the point.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound.

They tried it that evening, all five of them, standing in the dark near the fire making an appalling racket into the trees.

Nothing answered.

They did it again the next night, and something did.
