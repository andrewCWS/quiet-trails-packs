# Story 8 — The Last Shop

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Trip logistics · **Arc:** Quest
**Stretch words:** supplies, remote, final
**Feeling:** nervous, named once
**Word count:** ~1020

---

**NARRATOR:** There is a moment on every trip when the shops run out.

It doesn't announce itself. There's no sign saying LAST SHOP. You just drive through a town, and then another town with fewer things in it, and then a place that is mostly a name and a silo, and at some point behind you, without anybody noticing, was the last place you could have bought anything at all.

Billy had thought about this in bed, the night before, which is where Billy did some of his best thinking and all of his worst.

"When's the last shop?" he said, somewhere past the blue hills.

"Kandos, probably," said Dad. "Or Rylstone."

"Which one is *last*?"

"Depends which way we go."

This was not the answer Billy wanted. Billy wanted a number and a name.

Because here's the situation. Where they were going, there is no shop. There is no fuel. There is no bakery, no chemist, no bloke with a fridge full of milk. The nearest of any of those is back the way you came, which means that if you get to camp and discover you've forgotten something, the something stays forgotten for two days. That's what **remote** means. Not far. Just — nothing between here and needing it.

Kandos, when they got to it, was a proper town with a proper main street: a wide one, wider than it needed to be, with big old buildings along it that looked like they'd been built for more people than currently lived there. Which is exactly what happened. Kandos was built for the cement works — a whole town made on purpose, in one go, for the people who worked at the enormous factory up the road. The chimney is still there. You can see it from a long way off.

They parked. And Billy got out of the car with his list.

"Right," he said.

"We're getting ice," said Dad.

"We're getting ice," said Billy, "*and.*"

The and took eleven minutes and involved the whole family, and it went like this.

Rori wanted lollies. Rori was told no. Rori then wanted lollies in a strategic sort of way, from a different angle, and was told no again, with respect.

Skye read out every single thing on one shelf.

Adi found the doors that open by themselves and used them fourteen times.

And Billy went down his list, out loud, item by item, checking things off against what was actually in the car — which is a completely different exercise from checking things off against your own memory, and takes about four times as long, and catches things.

It caught two.

The first was matches. There were matches in the trailer, but only one box, and the box felt light, and Billy did not like a box that felt light. He bought another. That box of matches would go on to start every fire on the trip, because the first box turned out to be nearly all used-up ones that somebody had put back in, which is a crime.

The second was harder to explain, and Billy nearly didn't say it.

There's a tight, fluttery feeling you get when you think you might be making a fuss over nothing, and you have to decide whether to say the thing anyway. That feeling is called being **nervous**. Billy stood in the aisle with it for a second, and then said it.

"We haven't got anything for if someone gets a splinter."

Everybody stopped.

"We've got bandaids," said Rori.

"A bandaid doesn't get it *out.*"

And that is how the Hicks family came to own a pair of tweezers from a small shop in Kandos, and how, on the second afternoon, when Rori got a piece of a fence post the size of a grass seed into the side of her thumb, it took forty seconds and one held breath instead of being a two-day problem in a place with no chemist.

Billy did not say I told you so. He thought it very loudly. But he didn't say it, and that's the same thing as being kind, most of the time.

They loaded the **supplies** into the boot. Ice, matches, tweezers, bread, and lollies, because Rori's fourth angle of attack worked.

And as they pulled out of the wide main street, Billy turned right around in his seat and watched the shops go past and get smaller: a bakery, a place with tools in the window, a pub with an upstairs balcony, and then a corner, and then houses, and then paddocks.

He never worked out which one was **final**. That's the thing about the last shop — you only ever know afterwards.

So here's a mission, and it's for the drive.

Watch for the last shop. Any shop. A servo counts, a bakery counts, a shed with a sign counts.

When you're sure you've passed it — when there's been nothing but paddocks for long enough that you're certain — say its name out loud.

And then keep watching. Because the thing about being sure you've passed the last shop is that quite often, about ten minutes later, there's one more.
