# Story 26 — Six Legs and a Thousand Eyes

**Matrix:** story 24 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** Insects & small things · **Arc:** Transformation
**Stretch words:** nymph, split, hover
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** The empty insect was still holding on.

That's what stopped Skye — not the dragonfly, which came later, but the thing clamped to a reed about a hand's width above the water, in the sun, perfectly still.

It was insect-shaped. Six legs, gripping. A head with big blank eyes. A body about as long as her thumb.

And it was hollow. There was a split up the back of it, a clean tear from the shoulders to the top of the head, and through the split you could see straight into an empty shell. It was the colour of weak tea and light enough to move when she breathed on it.

"It's *gone,*" said Skye. "It's still holding on and it's *gone,* there's nothing in it, come and look, come and look —"

Billy came and looked and did not touch it, which was correct, because it comes to pieces.

Here's what they were looking at, and it's one of the great stories in the valley.

That reed is standing in the water, and under the water — for a long time, sometimes a whole year, sometimes two — a dragonfly is not a dragonfly. It's a **nymph**: a squat brown six-legged thing walking about on the bottom, in the weed and the silt, hunting.

And it's a serious hunter. It has a lower lip that shoots out on a hinge, faster than you can see, and grabs. It eats tadpoles. It eats other insects. Small fish are not safe.

It does that for a year or more. Growing, moulting, growing again. Never once seeing the sky properly.

Then one morning it climbs.

It comes up a reed, out of the water for the first time in its life, and grips, and stops. And then the skin **splits** up its back, and something completely different starts pulling itself out — soft, pale, crumpled, with four wet wings folded up like screwed-up paper.

It hangs there while blood pumps into the wings and pushes them flat, and it dries, and it hardens, and the colour comes.

Then it flies away and never touches the water again except to lay eggs in it.

And it leaves the empty skin behind, still gripping the reed, because the legs locked on before the split and nothing ever unlocked them.

Skye sat down on the bank and thought about this for a long time.

"So it was a different animal," she said.

Same animal. Two completely different lives, and it doesn't remember the first one, because it doesn't have the equipment to.

"That's the *best thing I've ever heard.*"

They found eleven more empty skins in the next half hour, all along the reeds in that one bay, all facing the same way, all still holding on. Rori found one with the split only halfway, where something had gone wrong, and put it back exactly where it was without being asked.

And then they got the other half of the story, because a dragonfly came.

Big — as long as Billy's finger — with a body that was blue at one end and dark at the other, and four separate wings that beat out of time with each other. It **hovered** about a metre from Skye's face, absolutely still in the air, in a way that no bird can do and no helicopter can do for long.

Then it went sideways. Then backwards. Then it stopped again.

Four wings, each one working on its own, is why. It can push in any direction it likes, including straight back, and it can hold itself in one spot in a breeze all afternoon.

And it was watching them. Properly watching. A dragonfly's eye is made of thousands of separate lenses packed into a dome, and the dome goes nearly all the way round its head, which means it can see almost everywhere at once without turning.

"It can see behind itself," said Billy.

It can see behind itself.

"That's not *allowed* either," said Billy, who had been having a week of this.

**ECHO:** It's been up there since the water warmed.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing in the reeds where the mud starts. That's Echo.

**ECHO:** They come out in the mornings, mostly. If you sit here early you'll see one doing it.

**NARRATOR:** "How long does it take?" said Skye.

**ECHO:** Longer than you'll sit.

**NARRATOR:** "How long?"

**ECHO:** An hour. More. Everybody asks and nobody stays.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it's a finding one.

Go along the reeds where they come out of the water. Look about a hand's width above the surface, on the stems, on the sunny side.

Find a shed skin. Don't pull it off — it'll come to bits. Just look at the split up the back, and look at the legs still holding on.

Then count how many you can find in one small patch. There are always more than you think, and everybody misses the first four.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone.

Skye's record was nineteen. Billy disputed six of them on the grounds that they were bits of reed, and he was right about two.
