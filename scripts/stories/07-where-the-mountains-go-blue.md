# Story 7 — Where the Mountains Go Blue

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Rock & sky · **Arc:** How-it-works
**Stretch words:** scatter, haze, distant
**Feeling:** not named in this one — see the ratio rule
**Word count:** ~1000

---

**NARRATOR:** The mountains were the wrong colour.

Billy noticed it first, out of the left-hand window, about an hour and a half in. The trees beside the road were green, ordinary green, the green trees have always been. But the hills further away weren't green at all. They were grey-green. And the ones behind those were blue-grey. And the ones behind *those*, right at the back where the land met the sky, were blue. Properly blue. Blue like the sea in a picture, blue like something you'd have to paint with a completely different paint.

"The far ones are blue," said Billy.

"They're mountains," said Rori. "Mountains are blue."

"But they're the *same trees.*"

And they were. That was the thing that wouldn't leave him alone. Nobody had planted blue trees on the far hills and green ones on the near hills. It was one forest, going on and on, and it changed colour depending on how far away you were standing.

"Maybe they're painted," said Skye. "Maybe there's a person who paints them, and they start at the back, and by the time they get to the front they've run out of blue, and then they use the green, and then they have to go all the way back and start again, and — "

"They're not painted," said Billy.

"They *might* be."

Here's what's actually happening, and it's stranger than paint.

Eucalyptus trees leak. On a warm day, a gum tree lets out a tiny amount of oil from its leaves — so little you'd never see it, but there are millions of trees, and it adds up. That oil hangs in the air above the forest in droplets far too small to notice, along with dust and water. Together they make a **haze**.

And here's the trick. When sunlight hits particles that **tiny**, the blue part of the light gets bounced sideways more than any other colour. It gets **scattered**. So blue light comes flying off in all directions, including straight at you — while the rest of the light carries on going.

The further away a hill is, the more of that hazy air is sitting between you and it. And the more air there is, the more blue gets scattered towards your eyes, until the hill stops looking like trees and starts looking like sky.

That's why the far hills are blue. You aren't looking at the hills. You're looking at the air in front of them.

"So it's not the mountains," said Billy slowly. "It's the *stuff in between.*"

"It's the stuff in between."

Billy sat back. Then he leaned forward again immediately, because he'd thought of the test.

"Then it should get bluer the further it is. Every time. Not just the mountains. *Everything.*"

So they tested it, which took the next forty minutes and was much better than the seat in front of them.

They found a tree by the road: green. A tree in the middle of a paddock: green, but slightly tired-looking. A line of trees along a ridge maybe a kilometre off: grey. A ridge behind that: grey with a bit of blue in it. And the far ranges, ten or twenty or who knows how many kilometres away: blue.

It worked every single time. It worked on a water tank. It worked on a shed. It even worked on a wind turbine, which went from white to a sort of ghost-white to nearly nothing at all.

"You can measure how far away a thing is," said Billy, "by how blue it is."

Which is exactly what your eyes have been quietly doing your entire life, without asking you, every time you look at anything **distant**.

Rori found the best one. There was a spot where three ridges lined up behind each other, one, two, three, each one paler than the last, like the same drawing photocopied and getting fainter.

"That one's the furthest," she said, pointing at the palest.

She was right. She hadn't been told which was furthest. Nobody had. She just knew, because everybody knows, because everybody's eyes have been running this experiment since before they could talk.

Adi looked out the window at all of it and said, "Blue."

Which was, on this occasion, the entire scientific explanation.

So here's a mission, and it's a looking-out-the-window one, so it starts now.

Find the furthest thing you can see. The absolute furthest — a ridge, a hill, a line of trees right at the back.

Now find something in the middle distance. And now something close.

Line them up in your head, and say what colour each one is.

Then watch for the moment the green stops and the blue starts. It happens at a different place on a clear day than a hazy one — so if you do this on the way home as well, it won't be in the same spot.
