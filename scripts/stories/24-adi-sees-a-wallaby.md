# Story 24 — Adi Sees a Wallaby

**Matrix:** story 21 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Refrain · **Key kid:** Adi · **Category:** Animals
**Stretch words:** none — this one is a freezing-still story
**Feeling:** none named
**Word count:** ~690 (~4:00). Refrain stories are short on purpose.
**Refrain:** "Freeze."

---

**NARRATOR:** Adi saw it first, which by now should surprise nobody.

It was late afternoon, the sort of light where everything goes gold down one side, and the four of them were coming back up from the water making an enormous amount of noise about a stick.

And Adi stopped dead in the middle of the path and put one hand up.

"Freeze," said Adi.

Everybody froze. That's the remarkable part. Four children, mid-argument, stopped moving instantly, because when Adi says freeze there is always something there.

About fifteen metres away, at the edge of the trees, standing in a patch of bracken, was a wallaby.

Dark — much darker than a kangaroo, nearly black across the shoulders, with a paler face. Smaller too, and rounder, and built low. It had a piece of fern in its mouth and it was chewing sideways, and it was looking directly at them.

Nobody moved.

The wallaby chewed.

Rori's nose itched. Rori did not scratch it, which for Rori was an act of enormous personal sacrifice.

The wallaby took another piece of bracken.

Here's the thing they were doing right without knowing it. A swamp wallaby's eyes are built for movement. Standing still doesn't make you invisible — it makes you boring. As long as nothing about you changes, you're a shape, and shapes are not a problem. The moment one of you shifts your weight, you become a thing that is happening, and then it's gone.

So they stood there like four badly made statues for about ninety seconds.

And this is the other thing worth knowing, which the bracken gives away. A swamp wallaby eats what almost nothing else will. Ferns, tough shrubs, bracken — plants that other animals leave alone because they're bitter or hard to digest. It also eats bark, and fungi, and occasionally things that ought to be poisonous, and it is fine.

Which means it can live where the food is bad. Which means it can live right next to a campsite full of people, quietly, all week, in bracken nobody looked at twice.

Then Skye's leg wobbled.

The wallaby put its head up, turned side-on, and thumped its back feet on the ground — one heavy double thump you feel more than hear — and went, in about four bounds, into the trees and gone.

"That was ME," said Skye, devastated.

That was Skye.

Everybody has been Skye. Everybody will be Skye again.

"Freeze," said Adi, cheerfully, to an empty patch of bracken.

They saw it twice more that week. Once at dusk near the toilet block, and once in the morning right at the edge of camp, and both times it was Adi who said it first and everybody stopped instantly, because by then it had become a rule.

Adi is two. Adi does not know the word "bracken" or the word "camouflage" or the word "wallaby", exactly — hers comes out closer to *wobbly*.

But Adi looks at everything, all the time, because none of it has turned into background yet.

So here's a mission, and everybody has to agree to it in advance, before you go anywhere.

Anybody can call it. Grown-ups too. The littlest person especially.

When somebody says **freeze** — everybody freezes. Not slowly. Instantly, mid-step, mid-sentence, mid-anything.

Then stay frozen while whoever called it tells you where to look.

Even if it's a leaf. Even if it's nothing. Especially if it's nothing, because the tenth time it's nothing, the eleventh time it's a wallaby, and by then you'll all be good at it.

"Freeze."
