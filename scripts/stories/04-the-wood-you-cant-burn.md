# Story 4 — The Wood You Can't Burn

**Matrix:** story 4 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** Trip prep · **Arc:** Small-one-saves
**Stretch words:** hollow, shelter, gather
**Feeling:** proud, named once
**Word count:** ~1020
**Note:** story 1 mentions the beetle in one line. This is the story where they
actually look.

---

**NARRATOR:** The log was perfect.

That was the problem, although nobody knew it was a problem yet. It was lying at the edge of the trees where the leaves went deep, about as long as a person and about as thick as Billy's whole body, grey on the outside and going soft at one end. It was dry. It was near. It was, in every way a five-year-old can measure, the best possible thing to put on a fire.

"That," said Billy, "is our log."

"That's our log," Rori agreed.

They got either end of it. It did not move. They got either end of it again, with more shouting, and it moved about a hand's width, and underneath where it had been the ground was dark and damp and completely different from the ground anywhere else.

Skye was already talking.

Skye had been talking since the car. She had talked about the gravel, and about a bird she saw that might have been an eagle and was probably a crow, and about whether you could dig all the way to the water from where they were standing, and about a stick shaped like the letter Y. So when she said "there's something under there," it arrived in the middle of a great deal of other information, and it went past everybody.

"There's stuff *living* under there," said Skye.

"It's a log," said Billy, heaving.

"No but there's — there's a whole —"

The log moved another hand's width.

"BILLY. *Look.*"

And something in how she said it made both of them stop and look, and once they'd looked they didn't get up again for quite a while.

Because underneath the log was a city.

There was a beetle the size of a thumbnail with a shine on its back like petrol on a puddle. There were three woodlice going in three different directions with great urgency. There was a spider that did not move at all and was clearly hoping not to be discussed. There was a pale, curled-up grub, fat as a peanut, lying in a little smooth burrow of its own making. There were threads of white running through the soft wood like the roots of something. And there was a small brown skink, who took one look at the four enormous faces above him and went from *there* to *not there* so fast that Rori made a noise.

And the log itself, when they looked at the soft end properly, wasn't solid at all. It had gone **hollow**. There was a tunnel up the middle of it, smooth-sided, wide enough to put your arm in, and something had been sleeping in it, because there were dry leaves pushed up at one end into a shape.

Here's what a fallen log is, and it's not firewood.

It's a **shelter**. A dead tree takes years to go soft, and years more to go **hollow**, and every one of those years it's a house. Beetles eat the wood. Grubs eat the beetles. Skinks eat the grubs. Birds and possums and gliders move into the hollows once they're big enough — and a hollow big enough for a possum can take a very long time to form. When you take a log away, you don't take wood. You take the house, and everyone in it, and the twenty years it took to build.

That's why you bring your firewood from home. Not because there isn't wood there. Because the wood that's there is busy.

Billy sat back on his heels.

"We nearly burnt a skink's house," he said.

"You nearly burnt it," said Rori.

"*We* nearly burnt it."

They rolled the log back. That took some doing, and getting it exactly where it had been mattered more to Skye than it did to anybody else, and she directed it — left a bit, no, *left* — until the log was lying in its own dent again with the damp dark ground hidden underneath.

Then they went and got the firewood out of the trailer, which they had brought from home in a bag, which had been on Billy's list, and which was frankly a lot less interesting than a skink.

That night, sitting by the fire that was burning wood from four hours away, Skye was quieter than usual. Not much. But some.

"I did say," she said, to nobody in particular.

"You did say," said Billy.

There's a warm, straight-backed sort of feeling that arrives when you turn out to have been right about something, and somebody says so out loud. That feeling is called being **proud**, and Skye had it for about half an hour and then got distracted by a moth.

So here's a mission, and it's a good one for when you get where you're going.

**Gather** everybody up and find one log. Just one. Roll it over — gently, and with somebody big helping — and count how many living things are underneath it.

Then, and this is the important half of the mission: put it back exactly where it was.

Because you weren't visiting a log. You were visiting everyone who lives there.
