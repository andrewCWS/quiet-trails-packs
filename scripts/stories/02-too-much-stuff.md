# Story 2 — Too Much Stuff

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Trip prep · **Arc:** Trickster
**Stretch words:** enormous, decide, spare
**Feeling:** disappointed, named once
**Word count:** ~1010

---

**NARRATOR:** Rori had a bag.

It had started out as a normal bag, the sort of bag a person takes camping. Then Rori had put things in it. Then she had put more things in it. And now it was sitting in the middle of the hallway looking like a creature that had eaten something bigger than itself.

It was **enormous**.

"What's in it?" said Billy.

"Everything," said Rori. This was almost true.

Inside the bag were: seven soft toys, three of them the same. A skipping rope. A jar with nothing in it, which Rori was planning to put something in later. A torch. Another torch. A blanket that did not fold, only bunched. Four books, one of them enormous itself, about horses. A swimming costume, a spare swimming costume, and a **spare** spare, in case of an emergency involving swimming costumes.

"That's a lot of costumes," said Skye, who had come to watch, and who then said several other things about costumes.

Rori sat on the bag. The bag did not close. Rori stood on the bag. The bag still did not close, and now it made a small unhappy sound.

Out in the driveway, Dad was doing sums.

That's a thing that happens before a camping trip that nobody warns you about. Somebody stands at the back of the car with a hand on the towbar, looking at a little silver plate with numbers stamped into it, and does sums.

Because a car can only pull so much. There's a number written on that plate, and it's not a suggestion — it's the weight the car and the trailer are built to handle. Go over it and everything still *looks* fine. It just takes longer to stop. And on a gravel road with a bend in it, how long it takes to stop is the most interesting number there is.

Which is why Dad came inside, and looked at the enormous bag in the hallway, and said the sentence.

"Rori. You'll have to **decide**."

Now. There is a feeling that arrives when somebody tells you that you cannot have all of a thing you wanted all of. It's a heavy, slow, sinking sort of feeling, and it lands somewhere behind your ribs. That feeling is called being **disappointed**.

Rori sat down next to her enormous bag and was extremely quiet, which for Rori was unusual.

"You could take the horse book," said Billy.

"I'm taking all of them."

"You could take three things," said Billy, who had a list and had been waiting all week to be helpful about it.

"*Three?*"

"Three's heaps," said Skye, from somewhere behind a cupboard. "Three's more than two."

So Rori did the sensible thing, which was to shout a bit, and then the actual sensible thing, which was to tip the whole bag out onto the floor and look at it.

And here's the strange part.

Once it was all out — spread across the hallway in a great sprawling heap, seven soft toys staring at the ceiling — most of it stopped looking like treasure. The spare spare swimming costume looked like a swimming costume. The jar with nothing in it looked like a jar with nothing in it.

She picked up the skipping rope. Put it down.

She picked up two of the identical soft toys and looked from one to the other, and you could see her thinking that if they were the same, then one of them was already there, in the other one.

She put one back.

"Torch," she said, and held it up. "Because it gets dark and I want my own one and not to share."

"Fair," said Billy.

"Horse book. Because it's four hours." She stopped. She looked at the heap for a long moment. Then she reached past everything else and picked up the smallest thing in it: a rock, grey, about the size of a plum, with a white line running all the way round the middle.

"That's a rock," said Billy.

"It's *my* rock."

"You're going to a place that's *made* of rocks."

"Then it can meet them," said Rori.

So that was three: a torch, a book about horses, and a rock going to meet other rocks. Rori put them in a small bag, and the small bag closed, all the way, with the zip going round in one go, which is a sound that feels much better than it has any right to.

The heap in the hallway got put away. Some of it went back on shelves. The spare spare swimming costume went in the wash, because it turned out it had not been clean.

And in the car, four hours later, with the road going bumpy and the dust coming up behind them, Rori had exactly three things.

She read the horse book. She turned the torch on and off eleven times, and Dad said something about batteries. And she held the rock in her hand nearly the whole way, and turned it over, and knew exactly where it was, which is a thing that had never once happened with the seven soft toys.

Sometimes a small bag is not less than a big one. Sometimes it's just a bag where you can find everything.

So here's a mission, and you can do this one right where you're sitting.

Pick three. Only three. If you were going somewhere for two whole sleeps, and you could take three things and nothing else, what would they be?

Say them out loud. And then say why the third one made it — because the third one is always the interesting one.
