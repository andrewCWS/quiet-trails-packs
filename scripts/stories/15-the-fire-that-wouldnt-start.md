# Story 15 — The Fire That Wouldn't Start

**Matrix:** story 53 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Camp craft · **Arc:** Patience
**Stretch words:** kindling, oxygen, gradual
**Feeling:** impatient, named once
**Word count:** ~1060
**Note:** matrix slot C3. Numbered 53 to keep published episode numbers stable.
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

**NARRATOR:** There was plenty of smoke. That was the maddening part.

Smoke means something is happening. Smoke goes up in a thin grey line and drifts across the campsite and gets in your eyes and makes you smell like a campfire, and it does all of that while producing no fire whatsoever.

Rori had been kneeling in front of it for eleven minutes.

She had built it herself, out of the wood they'd brought from home, and she had built it the way a five-year-old builds a fire, which is to say she had put the biggest and most impressive log in the middle, because that was clearly the main event, and then arranged everything else around it in a way that looked correct.

Then she'd put a match under it. Then another match. Then, in the end, most of a box of matches.

Smoke. Beautiful, useless smoke.

"It's not *working.*"

There's a hot, jiggling, unbearable feeling that arrives when a thing is taking longer than you decided it should. It gets into your knees and makes you want to poke something. That feeling is called being **impatient**, and Rori had it so badly she'd started rearranging the fire while it was on fire, which is the single most reliable way to put a fire out.

Billy sat down next to her, on the other side, and did not touch anything.

"How thick's the smallest bit in there?"

Rori looked. The smallest bit in there was about as thick as her wrist.

Here's the thing about fire, and it's the opposite of what everybody assumes.

Fire doesn't burn wood. It burns gas coming *off* wood. Wood has to get hot enough to start giving off that gas before there's anything for a flame to hold onto, and a thick piece of wood takes a very long time to get that hot — because all the heat you put in gets shared around the whole log.

A thin piece gets there almost instantly. Which is why a fire has to be built as a ladder.

At the bottom you want tinder — dry grass, paperbark, a scrunched-up bit of paper. Something that catches from a match alone.

Then **kindling**, which starts thinner than a pencil. Then thicker than a pencil. Then finger-thick. Then wrist. Then the impressive log, which goes on last, sometimes half an hour last.

Each layer's only job is to get the next layer hot. That's it. A fire is a **gradual** argument that you win one size at a time.

And the other half is air. Fire needs **oxygen**, and it needs it moving through the middle, which means the wood has to be stacked with gaps — leaning together like a tepee, or crossed like a little hut. Pack it tight and you get exactly what Rori had: hot, smoky, and going nowhere.

So they pulled the whole thing apart, which Rori did not enjoy, and started again.

Rori went and got kindling. She was extremely good at this once she understood what she was hunting for — the thinnest dry twigs, snapped off the underside of a fallen branch where the rain hadn't got, and the test is that it snaps rather than bends. She came back with three handfuls, sorted by thickness, without being asked to sort them.

Tinder at the bottom. Thinnest on top of that, leaning in. Then the next size. Nothing thicker than a finger anywhere near it.

One match.

It went up in about four seconds, and the sound it made was not a roar, it was a small crackling rush like something taking a breath, and Rori sat back on her heels and made a noise.

The impressive log went on forty minutes later and burned all night.

**ECHO:** You went and got the little ones.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing at the edge of the firelight where the leaves start. That's Echo. She'd arrived at some point during the forty minutes when Rori was sitting still watching a fire, which is one of the few things that will hold a five-year-old in one place.

**ECHO:** That's the hard bit. Not the match. The going and getting.

**NARRATOR:** "It took ages," said Rori.

**ECHO:** It took eleven minutes to fail and about five to work. That's usually the way round.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched twice at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and you do it before anybody strikes anything.

Go and gather the **kindling**, and gather it in order. Thinnest first — thinner than a pencil, thin enough to snap with two fingers.

Then a bigger pile. Then a bigger one. Three piles, sorted, before the fire is even built.

And then — this is the part nobody does — put the piles down next to each other and look at them, and see whether you've got enough of the smallest one.

You haven't. Nobody ever has. Go and get more of the smallest one.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound at all.
