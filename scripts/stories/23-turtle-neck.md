# Story 23 — Turtle Neck

**Matrix:** story 19 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Skye · **Category:** Animals · **Arc:** Unlikely friendship
**Stretch words:** sideways, hinge, harmless
**Feeling:** embarrassed, named once
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

---

**NARRATOR:** The turtle was crossing the road.

Not a road with cars on it — the dirt track between the campsites, which is a road the way a puddle is a lake. But it was crossing it, in the open, in the sun, a long way from any water, with the grim determination of something that has decided on a direction.

It was about the size of a dinner plate. Dark, almost black, with a shell like a slightly squashed dome.

"It's stuck," said Rori.

"It's not stuck, it's *walking*," said Skye. "It's walking, look at it, it's going somewhere, it's got a whole plan, it's —"

Rori picked it up.

She meant well. That's important. She picked it up under the shell with both hands, carefully, the way you'd pick up something breakable, with the intention of carrying it back to the water where it obviously belonged.

Two things happened at once.

The first is that the head came out — and it did not come out the front. It came out and swung round **sideways**, tucking along the inside of the shell, on a neck that was very nearly as long as the shell itself. It looked less like a turtle's head and more like a small dark snake had been living under there the whole time.

Rori made a noise that people talked about for two days.

The second thing is the smell.

It's hard to describe and impossible to forget. Sharp, and sour, and enormous for the size of the animal producing it. It got on Rori's hands. It stayed on Rori's hands, through the river, through soap, through the whole of the next morning.

She put the turtle down very quickly. The turtle did not appear to hold a grudge and carried on walking in the same direction.

That's an eastern long-necked turtle, and everything about that was normal for it.

Most turtles pull their heads straight back into the shell, front-first, like a fist going into a sleeve. This one can't. Its neck is far too long. So instead it folds it round to the side and lays it along the edge of the shell under the overhang — a **sideways** turtle, which is what a whole family of them are called.

The long neck is a hunting tool. It sits still on the bottom and then strikes out sideways, fast, at anything passing.

And the smell is its whole defence. No teeth to speak of, no speed, no claws worth worrying about. What it has instead is a set of glands that produce something so unpleasant that whatever picked it up puts it down again. It works on goannas, it works on dogs, and it works extremely well on five-year-olds. People call them stinkers, and they've earned it.

The turtle was also not lost. Long-necked turtles walk overland fairly often — moving between waterholes, or a female going off to find dry ground to dig a nest in. Carrying one to the water is the opposite of helping. It's picking somebody up in the middle of a walk and putting them back at the start.

Rori did not enjoy learning that part.

There's a hot, prickling, want-to-be-somewhere-else feeling that arrives when you did a kind thing and it turns out to have been the wrong thing, in front of everybody. That feeling is called being **embarrassed**, and Rori had it right up in her ears.

And here's where Skye did something that mattered.

She didn't say *I told you.* She had told her — she'd said "it's got a whole plan" about four seconds before it got picked up — and she was completely right, and she said nothing about it at all.

Instead she said: "Where do you reckon it's going?"

And they followed it. All the way, at a distance, for about twenty minutes, while it went under a fence, through some long grass, round a rock rather than over it, and down into a completely different bit of water on the other side of the campsite that none of them had known was there.

It went in without stopping and vanished.

"It knew the whole time," said Rori.

It knew the whole time.

**ECHO:** She's been doing that walk for years.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing in the leaves near the fence. That's Echo.

**ECHO:** Same line, more or less. I've heard her in the grass. She goes round that rock every time and I've never worked out why.

**NARRATOR:** "I picked her up," said Rori.

**ECHO:** I know. She's **harmless** and she's not helpless, and those are different. You'll remember the difference now, because of your hands.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it's a looking one.

Find a shell, or something shaped like one. A real shell, a curl of bark, a rock with a dome on it, half a seed pod.

Then work out which way a head would have to come out of it. Front, or round the side?

And if you ever find a turtle walking somewhere — let it walk. It's not lost. You are, a bit, and it isn't.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone.

Rori smelled her own hands about every ten minutes for the rest of the day, each time with genuine surprise.
