# Story 16 — Torches

**Matrix:** story 54 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Billy · **Category:** Camp craft · **Arc:** Overcoming fear
**Stretch words:** beam, adjust, pupil
**Feeling:** brave, named once
**Word count:** ~1060

**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.

**NARRATOR:** Everybody had a torch and everybody's torch was on.

That was the problem, although it took most of the evening for anyone to work out that it was a problem.

Four torches, four **beams**, swinging around the campsite, up into the trees, across each other, into somebody's face, into the fire, into somebody's face again. There was a rule about not shining it in people's eyes. The rule was broken roughly every ninety seconds.

And beyond the beams: black. Not dark. *Black.* A wall of nothing starting about six metres out, where the light stopped.

"There's nothing out there," said Rori, sweeping her torch across it.

"There's heaps out there," said Skye. "There's the whole entire bush out there, there's the rock, there's the water, there's about a million —"

"You can't *see* any of it."

"That doesn't mean it's not —"

Billy had gone quiet.

Billy did not like the black. He hadn't said so, because saying so makes it a thing, and if it's a thing then people are kind to you about it, and being kind to somebody about the dark is the fastest way to make the dark worse.

So he'd kept his torch on, pointed straight ahead, all evening. He'd walked to the toilet with it on. He'd sat by the fire with it on, which is a slightly odd thing to do.

Here's what nobody had told him, and it changes everything.

Your eyes have a hole in the front called the **pupil** — the black circle in the middle. It opens and shuts, all by itself, depending on how much light there is. In bright light it shrinks down to a pinhole. In the dark it opens up wide to let more in.

That takes time. Not seconds. *Minutes.* And there's a second part that's slower still: chemicals in the back of your eye rebuild themselves in darkness, and when they do, your eyes become extraordinarily good at low light. Properly **adjusted**, on a night with stars, you can walk around outside without a torch at all.

But it takes something like twenty minutes to get there.

And it takes about half a second of torchlight to undo the whole lot.

Which means that four children standing in the dark with four torches on had made themselves blinder than anybody there had ever been. They'd built a small bright room and then complained there was nothing outside it.

They tried it because Skye wouldn't stop going on about it.

Everybody off. All four. On three.

And it was awful. For about two minutes it was genuinely awful, and Billy stood with his thumb on the switch, and Rori said "nope" and turned hers back on and then off again, and somebody trod on somebody.

Then it got less awful.

Then the fire, which had seemed like a small orange thing, turned out to be lighting the entire campsite.

Then there were shapes. The trailer. The step. The line of trees, which was not a black wall at all but a ragged edge against a sky that was not black either but a deep grey-blue full of stars.

Then the ground. You could see the ground. Pale patches of sand and dark patches of leaf, and you could see well enough to walk.

Then Skye said "the rock," and there it was — the whole pagoda, standing up behind the camp, faintly, in starlight.

Nobody said anything for a bit.

There's a straightening feeling that arrives when a thing you were braced against turns out to be somewhere you can stand. It usually arrives after the hard part, not during. That feeling is called being **brave**, and Billy noticed it about four minutes in, standing in the dark with a switched-off torch in his hand, looking at a rock he shouldn't have been able to see.

"I can see the rock," he said.

"I know," said Skye, for once briefly.

**ECHO:** Better without them.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing about three metres away in the leaf litter, perfectly visible, which she had not been all evening. That's Echo.

**ECHO:** I've been here for an hour. You had a light on.

**NARRATOR:** "You were *there*?"

**ECHO:** I'm often there.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it, entirely unbothered by the dark.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it's ten seconds long and most people can't do it.

Everybody off. Every torch, at the same time, including the grown-ups, including the phone.

Count to ten out loud, together, so nobody cheats.

Then look up first, before you look anywhere else.

And if you can, stay off for longer. Every extra minute, you get more of it back.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound.

They stayed off for eleven minutes.

Rori kept her thumb on the switch the whole time, and never pressed it, and that counts.
