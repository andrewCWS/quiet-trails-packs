# Story 25 — The Gliders Come Out

**Matrix:** story 20 in the 50-story plan

**Mode:** Prose · **Key kid:** Rori · **Category:** Animals · **Arc:** Overcoming fear
**Stretch words:** glide, membrane, reflect
**Feeling:** not named in this one
**Word count:** ~1060
**Voices:** NARRATOR and ECHO. Echo sets the mission.
**Sound:** the boobook calls once, early, to set the dark.

**NARRATOR:** You have to go out into the dark to do this one, and there's no way round it.

Not to the edge of the light. Out — past the tents, past the last of the fire glow, in under the big trees where the ground goes soft with bark and the sky is in pieces between the branches.

(sfx: boobook)

Rori had said she'd go. Rori always says she'll go. There is sometimes a gap between what Rori says at four in the afternoon and how Rori feels about it at half past eight, and that gap was where she was standing.

"You don't have to," said Billy, who had noticed, and who said it quietly and only once, which was exactly right.

"I'm going," said Rori.

Here's what they were going out to do.

You hold a torch up beside your own head — right beside your ear, so the beam goes out in the same line your eyes are looking — and you sweep it slowly through the branches. Not fast. Slow enough to be boring.

And you watch for two dots.

Because the eyes of a lot of night animals have a mirror in the back of them. Light goes in, hits the mirror, and bounces back out through the eye a second time, giving the animal two goes at seeing by it. That's why they can work in near-darkness.

It also means that when your torch beam hits them, the light comes straight back at you. It **reflects**. Two small bright coins hanging in a tree, thirty feet up, looking at you.

This is called eyeshine, and once you've seen it you will never stop looking for it.

They found a possum almost immediately, low down, which was excellent and slightly disappointing because everyone had wanted the other thing.

Then Skye — who had been narrating the entire walk, including the parts where nothing happened — went silent mid-word.

"Up," she said. "Up up up up up."

Two dots. Much higher. Much further apart than the possum's, which is how you can tell before you see anything else, because a big animal's eyes are further apart.

And then it moved along the branch, and they could see it properly: a big fluffy grey-white shape, thick tail, ears like something drawn by a child.

That's a greater glider. And it is the best animal at Ganguddy that most people never see, because it lives high up and only comes out at night and does nothing loud.

It eats eucalyptus leaves. Only eucalyptus leaves. Which are tough, low in food value and mildly poisonous, so a glider spends most of its night eating and most of its day asleep in a hollow, conserving energy, because there is not much energy in a gum leaf.

And then it did the thing.

It went to the end of a branch, sat for a moment, and jumped.

And it didn't fall. It spread out — because a greater glider has a flap of furry skin called a **membrane** running from its elbows to its ankles, and when it stretches out, that skin becomes a sail. It went out and down in a long shallow line, silent, right over their heads, across a gap that must have been twenty or thirty metres, and landed against the trunk of a completely different tree with a soft thump.

A big **glide** can go a hundred metres. It steers by moving its legs.

Nobody said anything for a bit.

Rori had her torch pointed at the empty air where it had been.

"It *flew.*"

Not quite. Flying means going up, and a glider can only go down — every glide is spent height, and it has to climb all the way back up the next tree with its claws. It's not a bird. It's a possum that gave up on branches.

They stayed out for another twenty minutes and found four more, in three different trees, and one enormous huntsman which Skye discovered by putting her hand on it.

And here's the part about the dark, which is worth saying out loud.

Rori went out frightened. Not shaking, not crying — just quietly not wanting to, and going anyway. Nobody made a fuss about it, before or after, which is what made it possible.

By the end she was out in front.

**ECHO:** You were slow at the start.

**NARRATOR:** A brown bird with a long untidy tail was standing at the bottom of a trunk, in the torchlight, entirely awake, which lyrebirds mostly are not.

**ECHO:** I don't usually do the night. I came out for this.

**NARRATOR:** "Did you see it glide?"

**ECHO:** I've seen it a thousand times. I watched *you* see it. That's the better one.

**NARRATOR:** She scratched at the leaves, found something, ate it.

**ECHO:** Here's a mission, and it needs a torch and somebody big with you.

Go out after dark. Properly out, past the light.

Hold the torch beside your ear and sweep it slowly through the branches. Look for two dots.

Low and close together is a possum. High and wide apart is something better.

And when you've found a pair of eyes, turn the torch off and stand there. They're still looking at you. That's the whole point of the mission.

**NARRATOR:** And then she was gone, without a sound, in the dark, which is showing off twice.
